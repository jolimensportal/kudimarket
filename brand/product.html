The business, as decided.
Not as it was described.

Twelve decisions, taken in one sitting on 7 Oct 2026 and recorded here so nobody has to remember a conversation. Three of them overturned what the earlier documents assumed. KudiMarket is not a marketplace that matches strangers: it is the first vendor, buying and selling currency out of its own pool, with a hand-vetted vendor network around it. It trades seven currencies, not one. And it quotes its own rates rather than listing prices.

What changed, and why it matters
We are the counterpartyThe biggest change. KudiMarket buys currency from traders and sells to other traders out of its own float. That makes us a bureau de change and money-remittance business, not a matching platform, and it moves the first regulator to the Bank of Ghana with customer funds to be segregated from our own money.
Seven currenciesCedi, naira, CFA, RMB, dollars, pounds and euros. An earlier version of this work said cedi only, and another said exclude RMB. Both were wrong: the demand is a Nigerian in Ghana selling naira, and an exporter paying a Chinese supplier.
Our book, two currenciesWe hold and quote cedi and naira. Dollars, pounds, euros, the CFA franc and the Chinese yuan are all vendor supply from day one, and we never hold them. RMB was briefly added to our own book and taken back out: the flow barely happens, and holding it means carrying a Chinese-side receiving arrangement for a rare trade. Cedi is the pivot; everything is quoted against it.
A closed, vetted startVendors are admitted by hand, starting with the team and a short list of mobile-money agents who already trade this way offline. Automation comes later, once there is volume and capacity. A closed supply side is also the easiest shape to put in front of a regulator as a pilot.
Two promises, not one"Checked" and "KudiMarket handled". A checked vendor is one whose identity, accounts and history we verified, and we do not promise delivery. A KudiMarket-handled trade is one we took the other side of, so our money is at risk and delivery is ours. At launch, the team holds the second label.

One rule that follows from the company decision. A sole-proprietor registration in Ghana is not a separate legal person: the money goes to a personal account, our payer-name check must match that person's name rather than the trading name, and any claim against them is a claim against them personally. So the badge records the legal form and the exact account name that must appear as the payer.

What this does to the regulatory plan

The order has changed. The Bank of Ghana comes first, because the launch business is buying and selling currency with our own money, which is the activity the FX and remittance rules cover. The Securities and Exchange Commission matters second and specifically, because it covers the escrow route and because we hold traders' crypto. The sandbox application is still worth filing, and 4(5) still requires the pre-submission conversation before it, but it no longer gates the main business.

Three obligations follow from being the counterparty and none of them exist in the marketplace version: customers' money must be segregated from our own float and reconciled; every buyer and vendor is a customer to be identified, monitored and, where suspicious, reported; and our own treasury positions in three currencies are a business risk the licence application will ask about.

And nothing exotic is in our book. Cedi and naira both move by mobile money, which is why they are the right two. Every other currency, RMB included, is somebody else's inventory: the platform lists it, the vendor supplies it, and we never hold it. That keeps the treasury in two currencies, the licence narrow, and the Chinese-side question out of the launch entirely.

Still open, and the order I would take them in

  1. Whether dollars come into our book, and on what evidence: which currency a trader asks for most often that we currently cannot fill, and how often. This is the only plausible next currency, and it should be added to the board on volume rather than in advance.
  2. How a non-mobile-money leg reaches the trader, since dollars, euros and pounds do not arrive by MoMo: bank wire, card, wallet or cash hand-off, and what the trader is told to expect and when.
  3. The claim window and the dispute desk: how long a trader has to raise a problem, who decides, in which hours, and in which languages. Our own harm catalogue says this is the product's real internal surface.
  4. Identity: which KYC source and what liveness check, now that every buyer is a customer. Mobile-money registration names often differ from legal names, and our payer-name matching has to be designed with that, not bolted on after.
  5. Limits: per-trade and per-vendor caps, which are also the numbers we would propose to a regulator.
  6. Vendor-to-vendor fees: zero to grow the network, or a small fee that pays for the dispute desk.
  7. Entity and ownership, because it decides whether we are a foreign applicant under the 30% rule and what the Bank of Ghana application looks like.
